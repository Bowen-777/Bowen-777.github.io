Research

I am interested in AI agents and how they learn, reason, and interact. My current interests include agent evaluation and self-improvement, multi-agent and social simulation, world models, and multimodal/3D generative models.

I am also curious about model architectures and machine-native representations, especially representations beyond natural language. I have previously worked on graph learning and am open to exploring related problems across AI and machine learning.

Research projects

How Multiple Agents Explore, Interact, and Decide in Long-Horizon Dynamic 3D ScenariosOriginal project title: Research on Evaluating Autonomous Exploratory Reasoning of Agents in Complex Open Environments

Research in progress

Supported by Beijing Natural Science Foundation (QY26436; July 2026 – June 2027).

Bowen Fang (Research Lead)

I am building tasks in 3D social simulation environments to explore how multiple agents make continual decisions in dynamic open worlds. I generate 3D museum rooms and characters and connect both scenes and agents to a shared ground-truth backend. Agents control investigators, guards, or thieves, using different clues to recover or conceal jewels through cooperation and competition.

The backend checks actions and updates the world, while the 3D frontend displays these changes. Agents must revise plans as events unfold and present evidence as a graph. These tasks focus on decision-making through long-horizon, continual interaction, with trajectories potentially becoming a dataset for improving exploration and reasoning.

Key features
  • Finding evidence is part of the reasoning task. Agents choose where to go, whom to ask, and what to inspect, then adapt to new information. AER-arena follows the process from recognizing a gap in knowledge to completing a task.
  • Actions and goals interact over time. Characters pursue their own goals as the world evolves. Following someone, asking a question, or moving an object can affect later events, making timing and action order part of the problem.
  • Information comes from different sources and is unevenly shared. Maps, surveillance, observations, and memories refer to a common underlying world, but each character has access to only part of it. This creates opportunities to study evidence integration, cooperation, and information asymmetry.
  • Evaluation considers both outcomes and supporting reasoning. I record actions, observations, and state changes, and design evaluation around task outcomes, exploration, evidence-based reasoning, and rule compliance. This helps distinguish a correct guess from an effective investigation and successful action.
  • Environments, tasks, and evaluation are generated together. I am bringing spatial layouts, character goals, event relationships, and evidence paths into a generation process with explicit checks. The aim is to vary difficulty systematically and develop scenarios for evaluation and training.
Research perspectives

  1. Generality needs to be tested as tasks change. A high score on a familiar task is not enough to show that an agent understands how events unfold across space and time. I want to know whether its exploration and reasoning strategies remain useful with a different map, cast of characters, or set of information conditions. This is why I see open-environment evaluation as relevant to research on general intelligence.

  2. Sustained reasoning needs a place to organize and revise understanding. This project has made me think more about the agent harness—the tools, memory, and execution workflow that surround a model. It should help the agent maintain evidence, hypotheses, and unresolved questions. I am exploring workspaces and evidence graphs for this purpose, with a focus on whether agents can use, question, and update their records.

  3. Generated environments could support research on self-improvement. I would like to have one group of agents generate tasks and another attempt them, then use failures to revise strategies or generate new challenges. This could provide an experimental setting for recursive self-improvement (RSI). Tasks would still need independent checks of solvability, difficulty, and scoring, with progress tested on tasks held apart from the improvement process to avoid agents simply learning to satisfy one another.

  4. Interactions among participants raise questions absent from individual tasks. Different goals and information make cooperation, competition, concealment, and verification relevant to decisions. I want to study how agents divide work, when they share evidence, and how they collaborate with people. This also connects AER-arena to social simulation, where the assumptions behind simulated behavior need careful validation.

  5. Active exploration and the use of new information deserve explicit study. In my experiments, agents sometimes acquired important clues without changing their approach in time. I am therefore interested in whether they can identify what they do not know, choose valuable queries, and connect the results to existing evidence. Eventually, I hope agents can develop verifiable new knowledge through interaction with unfamiliar environments, extending what they can learn beyond the coverage of data prepared in advance by people.

  6. Interfaces and time budgets belong in capability evaluation. People can look, walk, and talk naturally; agents access the world through specific interfaces and spend time on inference and communication. I want to evaluate both judgment and timing, using controlled comparisons to separate limitations of the model from limitations of how it perceives and acts on the world.

The story behind the project & what I learnt

Where should an agent begin when a jewel disappears from a museum? It needs to decide which footage to inspect, whom to question, and when to act, while everyone else continues moving. I independently design and implement the system to study how agents actively seek evidence, revise their judgments, and turn reasoning into action in such environments.

At first, I focused on generating rich scenes and stories: giving characters their own goals and letting events unfold through interaction. Once I began running them, I found that a plausible story did not always hold together when an agent explored or intervened. I started asking where a character's judgments came from, what an action would change, and how a task could continue when events departed from the original plan. My attention shifted toward keeping the world's information and behavior consistent.

This led me to rethink the type system, data storage, and information flow. I wanted tasks to vary while keeping the source of each piece of information and the basis for each state change clear. As the system grew, I also learned to remove redundant concepts and unnecessary layers. Good architecture needs to accommodate change and define its limits. Those choices directly affect whether I can understand why an experiment succeeds or fails.

Working with real agents revealed another gap: acquiring information, remembering it, and using it well are different abilities. A clue could be available without prompting a useful change in behavior; adding memory or tools did not necessarily improve performance. I began tracing individual decisions: what had the agent seen, what had it missed, and why had it chosen this action? These observations drew me toward useful exploration, evidence use, and timely action. They also taught me to examine the experimental conditions before interpreting model performance.

Development changed how I work with AI tools, too. Coding and content generation tools made it possible to attempt a project of this scope, but locally plausible results could still fail when combined. I learned to give models room to create while defining the important constraints and acceptance criteria myself. When errors kept recurring, I began considering whether the representation or workflow needed to change, instead of only adding instructions to the prompt.

AER-arena now has a research prototype, and earlier scenarios have supported several real multi-agent experiments. I am still improving the connections among generation, execution, and evaluation. Next, I want to use more systematic controlled experiments to understand which designs help agents explore and reason, and whether the metrics capture those improvements. Through this work, I have gradually learned to turn an implementation goal into a question an experiment can answer.

The longer I work on the project, the more specific my interest in agents becomes. How do they recognize what they do not yet know? How do they divide work with people or other agents? How can failure help them improve their exploration strategies? These questions connect world modeling, multi-agent interaction, and continual learning. I hope AER-arena can provide an expanding experimental setting for studying them, and help me turn the difficulties I encounter during development into reproducible, testable research questions.

What I learnt

  • Understand information and state before building the system. I learned to ask where data comes from, who can see it, and who can change it. Clear types, storage, and information flow make the components easier to connect.
  • Set limits on flexibility and complexity. I have become more willing to merge overlapping concepts and remove abstractions I do not yet need, focusing on the parts that actually need to vary.
  • Keep responsibility for the overall design when working with AI. I learned to give coding agents clear responsibilities and acceptance criteria, while judging whether each local change serves the project's broader goals.
